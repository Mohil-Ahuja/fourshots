Four attempts, and where they go

NPCI allows exactly four executions per mandate cycle. Razorpay's documented retry policy spends them on consecutive days — which, for a debit that failed because the account was empty, means spending all four before payday.

The whole argument, in one picture

A ₹1,000 mandate due on 26 September. The account is empty; salary lands on 1 October. Both policies get the same four attempts and obey the same NPCI execution windows and RBI notice period.

This is the mechanical explanation for a documented industry figure: roughly 20 million UPI AutoPay revocations per month, driven by low customer balances.

Where it is worse

The headline is a net figure, and net figures hide things. The benchmark prints both of these on every run, so the trade-off travels with the result instead of being something a reader has to think to ask for.

Cash-flow lag

The engine waits on purpose — for money to arrive, for an outage to clear — so recoveries land later. A merchant feels that as delayed cash even when the total is higher.

Mandate-level regressions

Aggregate improvement is compatible with individual losses. 78 mandates worth ₹3,29,169 were recovered by the baseline and not by us — mostly customer-absent declines, where the engine escalates to a person while the baseline retries blindly and occasionally gets lucky.

Whether +49% recovered is worth a six-day mean delay is the merchant's call, not ours. Our job is to put the number in front of them rather than average it away.

It does not depend on guessing payday

An earlier version of the engine held a prior about Indian payroll and aimed each retry at the next plausible payday. It scored well — and collapsed to +9.9% when the world's payday moved three days while the prior stayed fixed. It was being told the answer more than reading the world. Spreading attempts evenly needs no payday belief at all.

How much of this is the AI?

About 1.8 percentage points, at the absolute ceiling.

Scheduling a debit is a money decision. A language model there cannot be tested exhaustively, cannot be audited line by line, and fails by producing a plausible-sounding wrong date — so attempt accounting, window legality, notice periods and AFA thresholds are all deterministic and hand-checkable.

The model does one thing a lookup table cannot: read the prose a rail attaches to a decline code the taxonomy has never seen. It proposes a classification, never a schedule, chosen from a closed set; the deterministic engine then does exactly what it always does with that class.

We measured its ceiling with an oracle that reads that prose perfectly. Any real model scores at or below it.

Deterministic constraint work +49.2% recovered vs baseline
AI triage, perfect +1.76% upper bound, not achieved
Unreadable codes 1.9% of the cohort

Inflating that number would have been the easiest claim in this project and the least defensible one.

What this cannot claim

  • The four-attempt cap may be per cycle or per mandate lifetime. Sources describe it as per mandate “identified by each sequence number”, which reads as per-cycle. The mandates-saved metric depends on that reading and it is not yet confirmed against the primary NPCI circular.
  • The cohort is synthetic. No public dataset gives decline-reason breakdowns for Indian recurring debits. Distributions are declared and pre-registered, sourced where possible, marked assumed where not, and swept where they matter.
  • One execution cycle is simulated, not a subscription lifetime. Multi-cycle effects — a saved mandate recovering next month — are argued, not measured.
  • Balance is a payday credit decaying monthly. It reproduces the mechanism that matters, but it is an approximation, not a cash-flow model.
